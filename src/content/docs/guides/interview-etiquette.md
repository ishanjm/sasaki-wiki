In Japanese corporate culture, an interview is not merely a test of technical qualifications—it is an evaluation of your sincerity (*Seijitsu*), manners (*Reigi*), punctuality, and cultural adaptability (*Tekiousei*).

Here is the essential guide used by **Sasaki Foreign Employment Agency** candidates to achieve high interview success rates with Japanese employers.

---

## 1. Physical Etiquette and Grooming

- **Dress Code**: Dark conservative suit (navy, charcoal, or black), white buttoned shirt, conservative tie (for men), neat formal footwear, and clean grooming.
- **Entering the Room (*Shitsurei Shimasu*)**:
  - Knock twice on the door.
  - Wait for the interviewer to say *"Douzo"* (Please enter).
  - Open the door, face the interviewers, bow, and say: *"Shitsurei shimasu"* (Excuse me).
  - Walk to your seat and stand beside the chair. Do **not** sit until instructed: *"Douzo osuwari kudasai"*.
  - Say: *"Shitsurei shimasu"*, bow slightly, and sit upright without leaning back.
- **Bowing (*Ojigi*) Angles**:
  - Greeting / Entry (*Eshaku*): 15-degree bow.
  - Respect / Formal Intro (*Keirei*): 30-degree bow.
  - Deep gratitude / Departure (*Saikeirei*): 45-degree bow.

## 2. The Standard Self-Introduction (*Jikoshoukai*) Template

Practice and memorize this structured introduction until it flows naturally:

> **Hajimemashite.** (Pleased to meet you.)  
> **Watashi wa [Full Name] to moushimasu. Suriranka kara mairimashita.** (My name is [Name]. I come from Sri Lanka.)  
> **Nenrei wa [Age] sai desu.** (I am [Age] years old.)  
> **Suriranka dewa, [Highest Education or University] de benkyou shimashita.** (In Sri Lanka, I studied at [School/Institute].)  
> **Nihongo wa Sasaki Foreign Employment Agency de mainichi isshoukenmei benkyou shite orimasu.** (I am studying Japanese diligently every day at Sasaki Foreign Employment Agency.)  
> **Nihon de [Nursing / Food / Construction / Engineering] no shigoto wo seikou sasetai desu.** (I want to succeed working in [Field] in Japan.)  
> **Donna shigoto demo isshoukenmei ganbarimasu.** (I will do my very best in every assigned duty.)  
> **Douzo yoroshiku onegai itashimasu.** (Thank you very much for this opportunity.)  
> *(Followed by a respectful 30-degree bow)*

## 3. Top 5 Questions Asked by Japanese Interviewers

### Q1: Why do you want to work in Japan instead of other countries?
- *Key Tip*: Focus on admiration for Japan's discipline, advanced technology, culture of safety, and your desire to build a long-term stable career. Avoid talking purely about money.

### Q2: How will you handle homesickness and living far from family?
- *Key Tip*: Emphasize independence, emotional maturity, ability to video-call family on off-days, and excitement to make new Japanese friends and colleagues.

### Q3: What will you do if your supervisor points out a mistake?
- *Key Tip*: Always say: *"Mazu sunao ni shazai shi, naze machigaeta ka wo kangaete, nido to onaji machigai wo shinai you ni aratame-masu."* (First, sincerely apologize, reflect on why the error occurred, and improve so as never to repeat it.)

### Q4: Are you healthy and capable of physical / shift work?
- *Key Tip*: Highlight your physical fitness, daily exercise routine, good sleep habits, and positive mental attitude.

### Q5: What are your goals after completing your work contract?
- *Key Tip*: Express your desire to continue advancing in Japan (e.g., passing SSW Level 2, qualifying for Kaigofukushishi, or becoming a team leader).

## 4. The Golden Communication Rule: **HOU-REN-SO (ほうれんそう)**

Every Japanese workplace operates on three foundational communication pillars:

1. **HOU - Houkoku (報告 - Report)**: Constantly reporting task progress, project completion, or unexpected developments to your supervisor without waiting to be asked.
2. **REN - Renraku (連絡 - Inform/Contact)**: Informing team members immediately of schedule changes, absences, delays, or work-related facts.
3. **SO - Soudan (相談 - Consult)**: Asking for advice or guidance before making an assumption or taking an irreversible action whenever in doubt.
